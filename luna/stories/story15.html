Story 15

Grandmother's Key

The next morning, Grandmother asked Luna to help her clean the attic.

It was a strange request.

Grandmother had avoided the attic for years.

“Why today?” Luna asked.

Grandmother looked at her for a moment.

“Because some things should not remain hidden forever.”

The attic was beneath the oldest part of the house.

Dust covered the wooden floor, and sunlight entered through a tiny round window.

There were trunks, old clothes, books, lamps, broken clocks and boxes filled with things Luna had never seen before.

Garo followed them upstairs and immediately began sniffing everything.

“He thinks there is treasure here,” Luna said.

Grandmother laughed.

“Perhaps he is right.”

They opened several boxes.

One contained old photographs.

Another contained letters from Luna's grandfather.

A third contained toys that had belonged to Luna's father when he was a child.

Then Garo stopped beside an old wooden chest.

He scratched the floor.

“Not that one,” Grandmother said quickly.

Luna looked at her.

“Why?”

Grandmother sighed.

“Because I have been waiting for the right time.”

She sat beside the chest.

From a small pocket inside her dress, she removed a tiny brass key.

The key was old and dark, but the mysterious symbol was engraved on its handle.

Luna stared at it.

“Is that one of the markers?”

“No.”

Grandmother placed the key in Luna's hand.

“It opens something your grandfather wanted you to find when you were ready.”

“Why me?”

“Because he believed you would listen when others could not.”

Luna looked at Garo.

“Listen to what?”

Grandmother smiled.

“The farm.”

She pointed toward the chest.

Luna inserted the key.

It turned with a soft click.

Inside was a collection of objects wrapped in old cloth.

There was a compass.

A small glass bottle.

A silver button.

A dried flower.

And a folded piece of blue fabric.

But there was no treasure.

“That's everything?” Luna asked.

Grandmother shook her head.

“Look beneath the cloth.”

Luna lifted it.

Underneath was a narrow wooden box.

It was no bigger than her hand.

The same symbol was carved into its lid.

Luna tried to open it.

It would not move.

“Does the brass key open this too?”

“No.”

Grandmother pointed to the objects in the chest.

“Your grandfather believed each one had a purpose.”

Luna picked up the compass.

The needle spun wildly.

Then it stopped.

It pointed directly toward the forest.

Garo barked.

From outside came the sound of wings.

A pigeon landed on the attic window.

Luna heard its voice.

“Someone is at the bridge.”

Luna hurried downstairs.

Grandmother followed.

At the edge of the farm, a horse was pulling a small cart across the wooden bridge.

There was nobody driving it.

The horse stopped beside the river.

Luna ran closer.

The horse looked at her.

“He left something behind.”

“Who?” Luna asked.

But the horse only shook its mane.

Inside the empty cart was a single envelope.

Luna opened it.

There was one sentence.

“The key opens the past, but the choice belongs to the future.”

Grandmother read the sentence twice.

“That is your grandfather's handwriting.”

Luna looked toward the forest.

“Did he know this would happen?”

“Perhaps.”

“Then why didn't he tell you everything?”

Grandmother looked at the brass key.

“Because he was afraid that knowing too much would put our family in danger.”

Suddenly, the little wooden box inside Luna's pocket became warm.

She took it out.

A thin line of golden light appeared across the lid.

It formed a second symbol.

The box clicked.

The lid opened by itself.

Inside was a single seed.

It was unlike any seed Luna had ever seen.

Its surface was silver, with a tiny golden mark running through the middle.

Grandmother stepped backward.

“That should not be here.”

“What is it?”

Grandmother whispered:

“A seed your grandfather promised never to plant.”

Luna held it carefully.

For a moment, the farm became completely silent.

Then every animal nearby turned toward Luna.

The horse stopped eating.

The birds stopped calling.

Even the insects seemed to disappear.

Garo stared at the seed.

Luna heard him clearly.

“It remembers.”

Luna looked at Grandmother.

“What does it remember?”

Grandmother did not answer.

She looked toward the old tree beside the river.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “we will talk about the little chest.”

Luna closed her fingers around the brass key.

The key was no longer cold.

It was warm.

Almost alive.

And somewhere beneath the farm, something answered with three slow knocks.

Knock.

Knock.

Knock.
